Abstract

The Scrolling Body
and What Remains

This mixed-methods pilot study examines the cognitive and affective dimensions of Instagram use through the lens of somaesthetics — the philosophical study of the body as a site of aesthetic experience and sensory knowing (Shusterman, 1999). Combining a preliminary survey (n = 53) with in-depth engagement sessions and interviews (n = 5), the study investigates what scroll-based platform use does to the body that performs it.

The central finding is one of ephemeral consumption: content is encountered, processed to some minimal degree, and forgotten — while affective residue persists. The pooled recall rate across five participants was 11.1%, with spontaneous recall averaging only 5.9%. Post-session affect was consistently negative: the session was rated worthwhile at just 2.25 out of 5 (the lowest of all survey items), and satisfaction at 2.51. Survey data reveals that 92.5% of participants use Instagram lying down, 81.1% open the app out of habit, and 62.3% feel largely unaware of time passing while scrolling.

Drawing on Foucault’s concept of disciplinary power and Shusterman’s three-part somaesthetic framework — analytic, pragmatic, and practical — the study argues that the scrolling body is rendered docile by platform design, and that this docility structurally forecloses the conditions for satisfying aesthetic experience. The study offers somaesthetic attention as both a theoretical and practical corrective.

Introduction

Platform-Mediated Scrolling
and the Failure of Somatic Attention

Social media platforms have become central to everyday life, often occupying significant portions of users’ daily attention. From the perspective of somaesthetics — the philosophical study of the body as a site of aesthetic experience and sensory knowing (Shusterman, 1999) — such affective outcomes may reflect a deeper failure: platform-mediated scrolling engages the body without satisfying it, producing stimulation in place of meaning.

This dissatisfaction extends beyond mood. Interactions with social media tend to produce what this study calls ephemeral consumption — experiences that are immediately engaging but leave little lasting cognitive trace. Unlike reading, conversation, or other embodied practices that generate durable knowledge, the scroll-based encounter with content arrives faster than it can be processed; attention is distributed rather than concentrated, and the body is held in a posture of passive receptivity trained into, in Foucault’s (1975) terms, docility.

The result is an experience that consumes time and attention while returning neither knowledge nor aesthetic fulfillment. The body is present but experientially absent — used without being lived in.

“The scroll-based encounter with content arrives faster than it can be processed — attention is distributed rather than concentrated, and the body is rendered docile.”

This study proceeds from a simple but underexplored question: not only how does Instagram use make people feel, but what does it do to the body as a site of sensory knowing? If, as Shusterman (1999) proposes, meaningful aesthetic experience requires embodied attention, presence, and intentionality, then a platform designed to suppress precisely these qualities does not merely risk making users unhappy — it structurally forecloses the conditions under which the experience could be satisfying at all.

Focusing on graduate students, this study combines quantitative survey data (n = 53) with qualitative engagement sessions and interviews (n = 5) to examine the somatic, cognitive, and affective dimensions of Instagram use in naturalistic conditions.

Literature Review

Doomscrolling, Well-being,
and the Somaesthetic Frame

Instagram, one of the most widely used social media platforms globally, offers an interface optimized for continuous, low-friction consumption: an infinite vertical feed, algorithmically curated content, and strong visual stimulation combine to sustain engagement while discouraging deliberate, evaluative attention (Mildner et al., 2024). Preliminary research indicates that despite the time invested in scrolling, social media use is frequently accompanied by negative affective responses — including guilt, dissatisfaction, and a perception of wasted time (Vicente et al., 2024).

Yet despite these negative affective responses, Instagram remains the second most popular social network in the United States (Rajendra-Nicolucci & Zuckerman, 2021). This persistence is itself a finding: if use were simply unpleasant, users would stop. Something more structural is at work.

The broader literature on social media use and well-being presents a more ambiguous picture. Valkenburg (2022), reviewing 27 studies, found that general, active, and passive social media use showed weak or mixed associations with happiness, life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety — while only problematic or intensive use consistently correlated with lower well-being. This inconsistency reflects the need for more precise, person-specific approaches that distinguish between types of use and types of outcome.

Psychological frameworks alone cannot fully answer these questions because they locate the problem in the individual rather than in the nature of the experience itself. Somaesthetics offers a more structural lens — one that asks what scroll-based platforms do to the experiencing body, not merely what the body reports feeling about them afterward.

Shusterman, 1999

Argues that disciplinary power operates through organization of space, time, and repetition into bodily routines so normalized they feel instinctive. The “docile body” framework applied here to platform design.

Combining ESM and log-data, demonstrates that mindless scrolling creates goal conflict and guilt, with measurable effects on daily well-being outcomes for regular social media users.

Methodology

A Mixed-Methods
Field Experiment

01

Preliminary Survey (n = 53)

Prior to the engagement task, participants completed a baseline survey collecting demographic information, self-reported weekly Instagram use (including screen time estimates), and contextual usage habits including posture, time of day, and physical setting. Participants also reported attitudes toward social media and typical affective responses following sessions.

02

Instagram Engagement Task (n = 5)

Participants engaged in a 15-minute free-use Instagram session on their own personal devices. Sessions were screen-recorded to document all content viewed, and participants were filmed from a fixed angle to capture postural and somatic behavior. To avoid priming effects, participants were not informed in advance that memory would be tested.

03

Unprompted Recall Task

Immediately following the session, participants were asked to list all content they could remember — spontaneously and without cues. This yielded the unprompted recall rate, a measure of what the body retains from habitual scrolling under natural conditions.

04

Prompted Recall & Affect Interview

Participants were then prompted to describe specific posts, themes, and accounts in detail. Following both recall tasks, structured reflective questions assessed post-session affect including feelings of guilt, satisfaction, dissatisfaction, and perceived productivity. Interview questions deliberately avoided leading language such as “guilt” or “satisfaction.”

05

Analysis

Descriptive statistics, frequency distributions, and Pearson correlations were calculated using Microsoft Excel. Qualitative interview data were analyzed thematically, with verbatim excerpts used to illustrate and support theoretical claims. Screen recordings were coded to count total items viewed per participant.

Analysis

Patterns, Correlations,
and the P4 Case

Habituated, Purposeless Use

Survey data points to a pattern of use that is largely automatic and without declared intent. 81.1% of participants reported opening Instagram out of habit, and 90.6% said they do so without a specific purpose — with 64.2% doing so often or always (M = 3.70/5).

This is consistent with Shusterman’s (1999) description of how deeply ingrained somatic habits override conscious will: the person does not notice this failure because habitual sense perception is “so inadequate and distorted” that the intended action feels as though it were performed. Likewise, 54.7% described their typical attention level as low — either noticing some content but skipping most, or scrolling without stopping at all.

“Like, it’s just instinct.”

— P4, post-session interview

Temporal Unawareness

Participants reported strikingly low temporal awareness during use (M = 2.47, SD = 1.08), with 62.3% scoring 1 or 2 — meaning nearly two thirds of the sample felt largely unaware of time passing while scrolling. From a somaesthetic perspective, what is at stake is not merely time management but somatic awareness itself: the capacity to be present in and attentive to one’s own bodily experience.

The P4 Case: Pace, Intentionality, and Recall

P4 represents the study’s most instructive case. With the fewest items viewed (32) and the highest individual recall rate (43.8%), P4’s pattern diverges significantly from the rest of the sample. P1, P2, and P3 — who viewed between 84 and 108 items — each recalled less than 8% of content overall.

Rather than classifying P4 as an outlier, this incident supplies the paper’s central empirical argument: scrolling pace may be a more significant variable in retention than volume of content encountered. Those who scrolled more slowly and encountered fewer posts had more time to dwell on each item, and the recall data reflects this.

P4’s anomalous result is also linked to the study’s methodological context. P4 explicitly connected the recording condition to heightened self-awareness and better recall:

“I was more aware of myself, which I’m not usually... because of the recording and everything. So, I was able to recall more and typically I just scrolled.”

— P4, post-session interview

This is, in Foucault’s terms, a panopticon effect: the awareness of being watched produces self-regulation. Crucially, it may offer empirical support for the somaesthetic argument: the absence of an observing gaze appears to be a necessary condition for doomscrolling to function as it does. Introduce awareness — through a camera, a research protocol, or deliberate somatic practice — and the behavior changes.

Somatic Absence

Qualitative data reveals a striking somatic absence during use. One participant described the experience as “an out of body experience” (P1). Another reported no awareness of physical fatigue during the session, only to notice afterward: “my eye is so tired and my neck. And my finger too” (P5). This delayed recognition is theoretically significant: the body was used, strained, and exhausted, yet remained outside conscious experience until the platform’s hold was released.

Discussion

The Docile Body,
Platform Power, and Somaesthetic Repair

The Docile Scrolling Body

Foucault (1975) argues that power operates most effectively not through overt coercion but through the organization of space, time, and repetition into bodily routines so normalized they feel natural, even instinctive. Instagram’s interface achieves precisely this. The infinite scroll, the algorithmically curated feed, and the frictionless one-thumb operation are not neutral design choices; they are, in Foucault’s terms, a disciplinary apparatus. The platform does not need to compel since it has already trained the body to comply.

Post-use feelings of the session being worthwhile scored the lowest of all survey items (M = 2.25), while satisfaction followed closely (M = 2.51). Yet participants continue to return. This suggests that Instagram use has become so automated that the platform does not need to make the session worthwhile to secure the user’s continued engagement. The body returns not because it was fulfilled, but because it has been trained to return.

Somaesthetics as Structural Critique

From the perspective of analytic somaesthetics, this dissatisfaction is not incidental — nor is it a personal failing. Just as Wysocki and Johnson-Eilola (1999) argue that “literacy” is often weaponized to locate failure in the individual rather than in the structures that produce it, discourse around doomscrolling framed as a problem of self-control or screen time risks doing the same. The docile scrolling body is not a personal failure; it is a political product.

Pragmatic Somaesthetics & the P4 Path

Pragmatic somaesthetics proposes methods for somatic improvement. The study’s findings suggest that slower scrolling, deliberate attention, and intentionality are not merely desirable attitudes but measurably consequential ones. P4’s 43.8% recall rate — more than four times the pooled average — was accompanied by greater reported self-awareness. Survey data further supports this: the correlation between intentional alignment and post-use satisfaction was among the strongest in the dataset (r = 0.591, p < .001).

“The docile scrolling body is not a personal failure. It is a political product — trained into compliance by a disciplinary apparatus designed to operate beneath the threshold of conscious attention.”
Analytic Somaesthetics

Names the Problem

Examines how platform design renders the body docile, encoding ideologies of passive consumption into somatic norms. Applies Foucault’s analysis of disciplinary power to the scrolling interface.

Pragmatic Somaesthetics

Proposes Methods

Suggests representational and experiential approaches to improving the quality of the scrolling encounter — slower pace, intentional attention, deliberate purpose. Empirically supported by P4’s recall data and the intentional alignment correlation (r = 0.591).

Practical Somaesthetics

Demands Embodiment

Moves beyond analysis to concrete bodily change. Illustrated by P4’s post-interview resolution to stop using Instagram lying down — a small but telling act of somatic reclamation requiring no therapeutic program or platform redesign.

Conclusion

From Analysis
to Embodied Action

Analytic, pragmatic, and practical somaesthetics together offer a layered perspective on how the body engages with Instagram — a scroll-based platform that occupies a vast and growing portion of daily life. Each dimension contributes something distinct: the analytic names the problem and its political conditions; the pragmatic proposes methods for improvement; and the practical demands that those methods actually be embodied.

The data from this study are unambiguous in their direction: sessions are felt to be worthless, yet users return; content is consumed in volume, yet almost none of it is retained; the body is exhausted by the encounter, yet this fatigue goes unnoticed until after the session ends. These are not incidental findings. They are symptoms of a systematic suspension of somatic self-awareness.

Notably, participants had already begun the practical dimension without being asked. Simply being invited to reflect on their bodily experience during the interview initiated a shift: P3 noticed their posture mid-session once attention was directed toward it; P4 remarked that thinking about posture and body experience “is something that is going to stay with me because I’m usually not really aware of it.”

Most strikingly, P4 concluded the session by resolving not to use Instagram lying down anymore. Having been brought into awareness of how bodily position during scrolling shaped what they attended to and what they retained, P4 made a deliberate decision to change it. This is practical somaesthetics in its most immediate form: not a therapeutic program or a platform redesign, but a moment of embodied reflection that produced a concrete intention to engage differently.

The somaesthetic take on doomscrolling need not remain an academic argument. It can function simultaneously as a framework for personal betterment, a basis for communal practice, and a political demand: one that shifts the burden of dissatisfaction away from the individual user and onto the structures that have shaped the scrolling body in the first place.

Finding 01

Memory is not the metric

High engagement volume does not translate to recall. The pooled recall rate of 11.1% — and 5.9% unprompted — demonstrates that content passes through the senses without leaving a meaningful trace.

Finding 02

Affect outlasts content

Negative emotional residue persists after specific memories have faded. Guilt, dissatisfaction, and perceived time-waste operate on a slower decay curve than episodic recall.

Finding 03

Pace is a cognitive variable

P4’s 43.8% recall rate demonstrates that the relationship between exposure and retention is non-linear. Intentionality and scrolling pace matter more than volume of content seen.

Finding 04

Awareness changes behavior

The panopticon effect suggests that introducing somatic awareness — whether through a camera, a protocol, or deliberate practice — is itself a meaningful intervention in habitual scrolling behavior.

Limitations

The sample is small (n = 5 for engagement sessions) and comprised of volunteers, limiting generalizability beyond the immediate sample. The naturalistic character of habitual Instagram use cannot be fully replicated even when the setting is carefully controlled. The absence of demographic variables such as gender was a deliberate choice, though such variables may have yielded additional nuance.

Video recording introduced an observer effect: participants reported that awareness of being recorded altered the scrolling experience. P4’s anomalous recall is directly attributed to this effect. While this complicates internal validity, it simultaneously offers a theoretically productive finding: the panopticon effect may provide empirical support for the pragmatic somaesthetic argument that introducing awareness is itself a behavioral intervention.

As an exploratory pilot, the findings are necessarily preliminary. They are intended to establish patterns worth pursuing and to demonstrate the significance of a somaesthetic framework for studying platform-based scrolling behavior.
